\begin{abstract}
In multi-core neuromorphic systems a core waits for neighbour spikes before each time step, and may skip the wait only if their silence is proven in advance. Trained networks are silent in most core-steps, but what makes silence provable has not been identified. Here we show that, for recurrent networks of adaptive leaky integrate-and-fire neurons with multi-tap synaptic delays, a $K$-step silence certificate needs a worst-case bound only for synapses with delay shorter than $K$, so the binding quantity is $R_{\mathrm{short}}$, the excitatory drive carried by those delays. Across 23 networks, certified silence decreases with $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ (Spearman $\rho=-0.804$), and total recurrent excitation does not order the networks. A certificate penalty meets the bound by moving excitation into longer delays: binding-tap drive falls 94\% while total excitatory mass changes 1--16\%. In two published Heidelberg Spiking Digits architectures the certified layer reaches 91.09\% and 91.46\% test accuracy against 91.30\% and 91.42\% for matched controls, certifying 89--94\% of the oracle fraction. The recipe transfers unchanged to Spiking Speech Commands and event-camera vision. An oracle bounds the gain of silence-based execution at $1.5$--$1.7\times$ in our networks; certificate-based execution, bit-identical to lock-step on up to 64 cores, realises $1.11$--$1.28\times$ at $500\,\mu$s per message. In a core routed in SkyWater 130\,nm, a 16-bit certificate with directed rounding runs beside the neuron update and keeps 96--99\% of certified silence, and on SpiNNaker certificates from the stored weights certify 91\% of silent windows without violation. Provable silence is a trainable property set by where excitation sits across delays.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Neuromorphic processors compute with sparse spike events and are scaling to systems of many chips, each with many cores \cite{kudithipudi2025,schuman2022}. In current processors these cores exchange spike events and most advance in discrete algorithmic time steps \cite{spinnaker,loihi,spinnaker2chip,darwin3}: before a core computes step $t+1$ it must know which of its presynaptic neurons spiked at step $t$. The dependency is enforced by a global barrier \cite{truenorth,loihi} or by local handshakes between communicating cores \cite{neuroscale,asyncaccel}. Runtime models and benchmarks of current systems identify synchronisation and communication as a principal cost that grows with system size \cite{loihi2runtime,neurobench,golosio2026}, so the time cores spend waiting for one another increasingly sets how fast a large network runs.

Trained recurrent spiking networks are sparse. In the networks studied here the mean firing rate is about 3\%, and whole cores are silent in roughly 59\% of core-steps. A core may use a neighbour's silence only if the silence is established before the step in question, because an unseen spike changes the computation. The quantity of interest is therefore provable silence: the fraction of core-steps for which silence can be certified in advance.

Since the late 1970s, conservative parallel simulation has used the minimum synaptic delay as a provable lookahead \cite{chandymisra,fujimoto,nest}, and topology-aware mapping lengthens it by placing long-delay connections between compute nodes \cite{lober}. Lookahead beyond the minimum delay requires knowing in advance that a group of neurons will not fire, and what makes such silence provable in a trained network has not been identified. We formulate per-core silence certificates for adaptive leaky integrate-and-fire networks with multi-tap synaptic delays. A certificate at horizon $k$ needs a worst-case bound only for synapses whose delay is shorter than $k$; contributions through longer delays originate at time steps that are already computed and enter exactly (figure~\ref{fig:mech}a). Provable silence is therefore limited by the excitatory drive arriving through short delays, $R_{\mathrm{short}}$, and a network can reduce it by moving excitation into longer delays without reducing total excitation.

Here we show that provable silence is limited by a single quantity that training can control. Across 23 networks certified silence decreases with $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ (Spearman $\rho=-0.804$), while total recurrent excitation does not order the networks (section~\ref{sec:governing}). A penalty on the certificate meets the bound by moving excitation from short to long delays (section~\ref{sec:mechanism}), with test-accuracy changes of $-0.21$ to $+3.03$ points against matched controls on the Heidelberg Spiking Digits (SHD), Spiking Speech Commands (SSC) and N-MNIST and inside two published SHD architectures, a learnable-delay network and a spiking transformer (sections~\ref{sec:accuracy} and \ref{sec:sota}); an adaptation-aware form of the certificate uses the exact adaptive threshold (section~\ref{sec:certs}). Certificate-based execution is bit-identical to lock-step, and an oracle that knows future silence bounds its gain at $1.5$--$1.7\times$ for the networks trained here, a bound that follows the length of silent runs (section~\ref{sec:execution}). A register-transfer core routed in SkyWater 130\,nm evaluates the certificate beside the neuron update in 16-bit arithmetic whose directed rounding keeps it sound (section~\ref{sec:hardware}), and on the SpiNNaker neuromorphic system certificates computed from the weights the chip stores hold on every window (section~\ref{sec:spinnaker}). Provable silence thus becomes a property that can be read from trained weights, trained for, and checked in hardware.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Synchronisation in neuromorphic systems.} TrueNorth advances all cores on a global tick \cite{truenorth}; Loihi exchanges barrier messages between neighbouring cores that flush in-flight spikes before the time step advances \cite{loihi}, and a recent runtime model of Loihi 2 isolates the per-step barrier cost as one of three bounds on runtime \cite{loihi2runtime}. SpiNNaker and SpiNNaker2 run cores against real-time timers and route multicast spike packets \cite{spinnaker,spinnaker2chip}, and mixed-signal processors such as DYNAP-SE2 operate without a global time step \cite{dynapse2}. NeuroScale replaces global barriers with local neighbour synchronisation while keeping execution deterministic \cite{neuroscale}, and asynchronous multi-core accelerators schedule each core from dependencies fixed at compile time \cite{asyncaccel}. Inter-node communication is likewise a principal cost in GPU and CPU simulators \cite{golosio2026,pronold2022,kurth2022,senk2026}. Removing layer synchronisation altogether changes the computation and requires training for the asynchronous schedule \cite{layersync}; silence certificates keep the computation unchanged and reduce how often a handshake must be waited for.

\paragraph{Lookahead in parallel simulation.} Conservative parallel discrete-event simulation lets a process advance when no event can arrive before a known future time \cite{chandymisra,fujimoto}, while optimistic methods roll back on causality errors \cite{jefferson}. Distributed spiking simulators take their lookahead from the minimum synaptic delay and exchange spikes once per minimum-delay interval \cite{nest,pronold2022}, and topology-aware placement raises that interval by mapping areas with long mutual delays onto different nodes \cite{lober,stacs}. A silence certificate is state-dependent and extends lookahead beyond the delay-derived value; our baseline receives the delay-derived lookahead in full.

\paragraph{Synaptic delays in spiking networks.} Delays support spike-timing computation \cite{polychron} and are now learned jointly with weights through dilated convolutions with learnable spacings \cite{dcls}, exact event-based gradients \cite{meszaros2025,delgrad,nowotny2025} and joint learning of delays and adaptation \cite{deckers2024}, including in recurrent networks \cite{delrec,balafrej2025}. Delayed kernels act as temporal attention \cite{bamsldk}, and delay networks have been deployed on Loihi 2, BrainScaleS-2 and RRAM-based dendritic hardware \cite{loihidelays,delgrad,denram}. Delay sets in this line include the shortest lags, which are the ones that carry the cost of provable silence.

\paragraph{Bounds and verification for spiking networks.} Bound propagation through membrane potentials has been used to certify and train spiking networks for robustness to input perturbation \cite{certsnn}, and robustness has been analysed through stability \cite{rsnn2024}, randomised smoothing of rate codes \cite{mukhoty2024} and the distance of membrane potentials from threshold \cite{wang2026}. Exact verification of spiking networks has been formulated with SMT solvers \cite{banerjee2023}. In conventional networks, training against interval bounds makes them hold through implicit regularisation \cite{gowal2019,mao2024}. We bound a different property, future silence of a group of neurons under recurrent input, and use it to make a scheduling decision safe.

\paragraph{Activity sparsity and event-driven execution.} Activity regularisation lowers firing rates and synaptic operations \cite{sorbaro2020,narduzzi2025,stanojevic2024}, and sparsity-aware hardware exploits inactive neurons through activity gating, event-driven cores and complementary weight and activation sparsity \cite{reeb2026,speck,hunter2022}. Energy comparisons show that spiking hardware gains only at high sparsity \cite{dampfhoffer2023}. Bounded fan-in is a recurring hardware constraint \cite{petschenig2023,dynapse2}, and partitioning a network onto cores determines its communication \cite{lober}. Event-driven reformulations reduce the work done under a fixed dependency structure \cite{eprop,paralif}; certificates act on the dependency structure itself.

\section{Theory}

\subsection{Network model}
\label{sec:model}

We consider a recurrent layer of adaptive leaky integrate-and-fire neurons \cite{lsnn,asrnn}. With membrane potential $v$, adaptation $a$, external input $I$ and spikes $s\in\{0,1\}$, neuron $i$ evolves as
\begin{eqnarray}
a_i(t) &=& \rho\, a_i(t-1) + \gamma\, s_i(t-1) \\
v_i(t) &=& \beta\, v_i(t-1) + I_i(t) + \sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}} \sum_j W^{(d)}_{ij}\, s_j(t-d) \\
s_i(t) &=& \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, v_i(t) \ge \theta + a_i(t) \,\right],
\end{eqnarray}
followed by the reset $v_i(t) \leftarrow v_i(t) - s_i(t)\,(\theta + a_i(t))$, where $\beta=\exp(-\Delta t/\tau_m)$ is the membrane decay, $\theta$ the base threshold and $W^{(d)}$ the recurrent weights for synaptic delay $d$. Setting $\mathcal{D}=\{1\}$ recovers the unit-delay recurrent network. Neurons are partitioned into $P$ cores of $C$ neurons; a core is the unit of synchronisation.

\subsection{Silence certificates}
\label{sec:certs}

A core issues a certificate ``silent through step $u$'' when none of its neurons can fire at steps $t+2,\dots,u$ whatever its neighbours do; a neighbour holding the certificate need not be waited for over that interval. Let $R^{(d)}_i = \sum_j \max(0, W^{(d)}_{ij})$ be the worst-case excitatory drive into neuron $i$ through delay $d$. Bounding the membrane forward from the measured state $v_i(t+1)$ gives a sufficient condition for silence over $K$ steps. If the drive is bounded by $R_i$ at every step, the reach converges to $R_i/(1-\beta)$, so
\begin{equation}
R_i < (1-\beta)\,\theta
\label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
is sufficient for silence over an unbounded horizon. We call $(1-\beta)\theta$ the excitatory budget; for $\beta=e^{-1/2}$ and $\theta=1$ it is $0.3935$. A finite-$K$ certificate starts from the measured membrane potential and is less restrictive than (\ref{eq:budget}).

Adaptation is non-negative, so the base threshold $\theta$ is a sound comparison. The exact threshold is also available: until a neuron's first spike inside the window its adaptation only decays, so its threshold at step $t+1+k$ is $\theta + \rho^k a_i(t+1) + \gamma\rho^{k-1} s_i(t+1)$, with both terms known when the certificate is issued. Ruling out a first spike against this threshold is as sound as ruling it out against $\theta$. We call this the adaptation-aware certificate and report it alongside the base certificate.

Certificates are evaluated by a fixed-point refinement. Starting from the set of all neurons that may fire within the window, each neuron's reach is bounded using only the drive attributable to that set, which yields a possibly smaller set; iterating to a fixed point gives the certified set. The refinement is sound for any initial superset.

\subsection{The delay decomposition}
\label{sec:decomposition}

Consider a certificate rooted after step $t+1$ and a horizon $k\ge 1$, so the step under consideration is $\tau = t+1+k$. A synapse with delay $d$ contributes from step $\tau - d = t + 1 + k - d$. If $d \ge k$ that step is already computed, its spikes are known and the contribution enters exactly. Only synapses with $d < k$ draw on future steps and need a worst-case bound, so the quantity to bound at horizon $k$ is $\sum_{d<k} R^{(d)}_i$, and for a $K$-step certificate
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{short}} = \sum_{d<K} R^{(d)} .
\end{equation}
Two consequences follow. A minimum delay $d_{\min}$ gives $d_{\min}$ steps of exact lookahead at no cost, the lookahead that conservative parallel simulation extracts from minimum delay \cite{fujimoto,nest}; certificates extend it. And $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ can be reduced without reducing total excitation by moving excitatory mass from short to long delays: with $\mathcal{D}=\{2,4,8\}$ and $K=4$ only the $d=2$ tap is bound. Figure~\ref{fig:mech} summarises the decomposition. In panel a, a certificate rooted after step $t+1$ asks whether step $\tau$ can fire; a synapse of delay $d$ supplies that step from $\tau-d$, which is a computed step when $d\geq k$ and lies inside the horizon when $d<k$. Panel b shows excitatory mass against delay for the network with per-synapse learnable delays on a logarithmic scale, and panel c the worst-case reach $\beta^k v + R_{\mathrm{short}}(1-\beta^k)/(1-\beta)$ at the measured $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ of the control and constrained networks of table~\ref{tab:governing}. With certificates a core advances through the certified horizon instead of waiting once per step (panel d).

\subsection{Soundness}
\label{sec:soundness}

The certificate combines the two preceding subsections. Consider a certificate rooted after step $t+1$, with the external input $I_i$ known over the horizon, as it is when the input pathway is feedforward (section~\ref{sec:networks}). For a set $S$ of neurons that may fire in the window and $k=1,\dots,K$, define the reach
\begin{equation}
U_i(k) = \beta\,U_i(k-1) + I_i(t+1+k) + \sum_{d\ge k}\sum_j W^{(d)}_{ij}\,s_j(t+1+k-d) + \sum_{d<k}\sum_{j\in S}\max\!\left(0,W^{(d)}_{ij}\right),
\label{eq:reach}
\end{equation}
with $U_i(0)=v_i(t+1)$, and the threshold bound $\Theta_i(k)=\theta+\rho^k a_i(t+1)+\gamma\rho^{k-1}s_i(t+1)$ of the adaptation-aware certificate, or $\Theta_i(k)=\theta$ for the base certificate.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:sound}
Let $F$ be the set of neurons that spike at some step in $t+2,\dots,t+1+K$. If $F\subseteq S$, every neuron $i\in F$ has $U_i(k)\ge\Theta_i(k)$ for some $k\le K$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\textit{Proof.} Let $i\in F$ and let $t+1+k^\ast$ be its first spike in the window. For $k<k^\ast$ neuron $i$ does not spike, so its membrane is not reset and its adaptation decays without increments, which gives $a_i(t+1+k)=\Theta_i(k)-\theta$ for $k\le k^\ast$ under the adaptation-aware bound. The recurrent drive at step $t+1+k$ comes from steps $t+1+k-d$. For $d\ge k$ these steps are at most $t+1$, so their spikes are known and enter (\ref{eq:reach}) exactly. For $d<k$ they lie in $t+2,\dots,t+k$, where only neurons of $F\subseteq S$ spike, and $W^{(d)}_{ij}s_j\le\max(0,W^{(d)}_{ij})$ for $s_j\in\{0,1\}$. Since $\beta>0$, induction on $k$ gives $v_i(t+1+k)\le U_i(k)$ for $k\le k^\ast$. At $k^\ast$ the spike means $v_i(t+1+k^\ast)\ge\theta+a_i(t+1+k^\ast)$, so $U_i(k^\ast)\ge\Theta_i(k^\ast)$; because $a_i\ge0$, the same holds for the base bound $\Theta_i=\theta$. $\square$

With $S$ the set of all neurons, a core none of whose neurons reaches its threshold bound for any $k\le K$ is therefore silent through step $t+1+K$. The fixed-point refinement of section~\ref{sec:certs} sets $S_0$ to all neurons and $S_{n+1}$ to the neurons that reach their threshold bound under $S_n$. If $F\subseteq S_n$, the proposition gives $F\subseteq S_{n+1}$, so every iterate, and the fixed point, contains $F$, and a core disjoint from the fixed point is silent. The guarantee holds for any weights, external input and neighbour activity; training changes how often a certificate can be issued, not whether it holds.

\subsection{Training objective}

Certificates are made to hold by adding a penalty on the worst-case $K$-step reach, evaluated on windows in which the network is silent:
\begin{equation}
L = L_{\mathrm{task}} + \lambda \cdot \mathrm{mean}\!\left[\, \mathrm{relu}\!\left(\max_k \mathrm{reach}_k - \theta\right) \cdot \mathbf{1}[\mathrm{silent}] \,\right],
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{reach}_k$ uses the exact and bounded terms of section~\ref{sec:decomposition}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Datasets and evaluation protocol}

We use SHD (20 classes, 8\,156 training and 2\,264 test samples) and SSC (35 classes, 75\,466 training samples) \cite{shd}, and N-MNIST \cite{nmnist}. For our own architecture, input spikes over 700 channels are binned into $T=100$ steps spanning 1.4\,s. The SHD test speakers include speakers absent from training, so we hold out training speakers 3 and 6 (1\,169 samples) as a speaker-disjoint validation set, select every architecture, hyperparameter and epoch on it, and evaluate the test set once per run on fresh seeds; recent work on these benchmarks adopts validation-based selection for the same reason \cite{nowotny2025,meszaros2025,baronig2025,moro2026,delrec}. Each accuracy change is computed per seed against a control with identical architecture, schedule and seed trained without the penalty.

\subsection{Networks}
\label{sec:networks}

Our reference architecture has $H=512$ neurons in 16 cores of 32, $\beta=e^{-1/2}$, $\theta=1$, $\rho=e^{-14/200}$, $\gamma=0.02$. We compare a unit-delay network ($\mathcal{D}=\{1\}$), multi-tap networks with $\mathcal{D}=\{1,2,4,8\}$ and $\{2,4,8\}$, and a network with a learnable delay per synapse, $D_{ij}\in[2,8]$, learned jointly with the weights \cite{dcls}. Locally connected variants restrict recurrence to neighbouring 32-neuron groups on a ring (fan-in 96).

For the host-network comparison the same recurrent layer is inserted into two published SHD networks using the authors' released code and configuration: the learnable-delay network of \cite{dcls}, two feedforward layers of 256 neurons with one learnable delay per synapse up to 250\,ms, and the spiking transformer SpikCommander \cite{spikcommander}. The layer sits between the host's last hidden stage and its readout. Its external current is a linear map of the host's spikes followed by batch normalisation and depends only on the input, so the certificate treats it as known; the readout lies downstream of the recurrence. In SpikCommander the recurrent layer trains at a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ while every host parameter keeps the reference rate of $10^{-2}$.

\subsection{Training}

Networks are implemented in PyTorch and trained on single NVIDIA GeForce RTX GPUs. The reference architecture is trained with surrogate gradients and AdamW (learning rate $2\times10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch 128, cosine schedule, 150 epochs) with circular shifts in time and channel and random channel and time masking. Constrained networks are fine-tuned from their matched controls for 150 epochs at $5\times10^{-4}$, with $\lambda$ ramped linearly over the first half of training; $\lambda=1$ unless stated. On SSC and N-MNIST the recipe is transferred unchanged, with the number of epochs matched to SHD by gradient steps. Host networks are trained with the authors' recipes; their certified layers are fine-tuned for 100 epochs with $\lambda=1$ and selected on validation among epochs at full penalty weight.

\subsection{Certification measurement}

We report the certified core fraction, the proportion of core-steps for which a whole core is provably silent over the next $K=4$ steps, and the oracle fraction, the proportion of core-steps in which the core is actually silent over the same window. A violation is a certified window in which a neuron fires; violations are counted on every evaluation, for both certificates. A silent run is a maximal sequence of consecutive steps in which no neuron of a core spikes; run lengths are measured on 50 test samples with 16 cores of 32 neurons.

\subsection{Execution engines}

A shared-memory engine in C++20 runs one thread per core with an emulated per-message latency $L$, and a second engine runs two processes exchanging frames over TCP. In both, the handshake baseline receives the lookahead the minimum delay provides: to compute step $t+1$ a core waits only for neighbour spikes up to step $t+1-d_{\min}$. Spike trains are compared bit for bit against a single-threaded reference on every run. An oracle mode tells each core its true future silent run, read from the reference spike trains and capped at the certificate's 16-step horizon; it bounds the gain of any silence-based scheme. Timings were taken on an otherwise idle machine. The 64-core measurements use a two-socket Intel Xeon Platinum 8375C server, with one thread per physical core.

\subsection{Hardware implementation}
\label{sec:hwmethods}

One core of 32 neurons was described at register-transfer level in Amaranth and exported to Verilog. State is fixed point with 12 fractional bits in 24-bit words, and decay factors, threshold and adaptation gain are programmable. A time-multiplexed neuron unit updates one neuron per clock cycle. Synaptic events add their weights into delay accumulators that form a ring of 16 steps split into four banks by slot modulo four, so the four slots of a certificate window are read in one cycle. The certificate unit evaluates the bound of section~\ref{sec:certs} for the neuron being updated, from the same state and accumulator reads, in four pipelined stages. Because it computes an upper bound, it runs on 16-bit saturating operands in units of $2^{-8}$: membrane, input and bounded drive are rounded up, decay factors are rounded to six fractional bits in the conservative direction, and adaptation is rounded down. Every operation is monotone in its operands and rounds in the conservative direction, so the reduced-precision reach is at least the full-precision reach of (\ref{eq:reach}) and the reduced-precision threshold bound at most the full-precision one; saturation at the lower limit raises the reach, and saturation at the upper limit occurs only above the largest threshold bound, where the stage has already flagged a possible spike. A core certified at this precision is therefore silent by proposition~\ref{prop:sound}. Once a neuron of the core can fire, the core's certificate has failed and the stage registers are held for the rest of the pass. A certified core performs no neuron updates in the following $K-2$ steps and then advances each neuron through the $K-1$ skipped steps in $K-1$ consecutive cycles of the neuron unit, with one state read and one state write per neuron.

A fixed-point reference model with the same arithmetic agrees with the floating-point engine on 99.9\% of spikes. Cycle-level simulation of every core was compared against it at every time step. The neuron and certificate units were synthesised with Yosys onto the SkyWater 130\,nm high-density standard-cell library (typical corner, 25\,$^{\circ}$C, 1.8\,V) and placed, clock-tree synthesised, repaired and routed with OpenROAD \cite{openroad} at a 24\,ns clock. Switching activity was measured by gate-level simulation of each routed netlist with Icarus Verilog on 5\,000 cycles of operands taken from the trained networks, and power was computed from the routed design at the measured mean activity; energy per operation is this power times the clock period.

\subsection{Deployment on SpiNNaker}
\label{sec:spinmethods}

The locally connected $K=4$ network was deployed on a 48-chip SpiNNaker board \cite{spinnaker} through the EBRAINS research infrastructure, using PyNN and sPyNNaker \cite{pynn,spynnaker}. Each neuron is a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron with instantaneous synapses and reset to zero (\texttt{IF\_curr\_delta}), the ALIF neuron of section~\ref{sec:model} without adaptation. Membrane decay was set to the network's $\beta$ and confirmed on the chip, and weights were scaled by the measured response to a single input spike. The delays of the input pathway and of the three recurrent taps were set from the measured arrival times of single spikes, so that a spike emitted at step $t$ reaches its targets at step $t+d$. The board ran 100 times slower than real time with 16 neurons per processor core, which gives each core time to receive every input spike of a step. Fifty SHD test samples were presented in sequence with 30 steps of silence between them, and spikes and membrane potentials of all 512 neurons were recorded at every step. After loading, the weights stored on the chip were read back. Certificates were computed for every core of 32 neurons and every step from the recorded membranes and spikes with $\Theta_i=\theta$ and $S$ the set of all neurons, and a certified window in which a neuron of the core spiked on the chip was counted as a violation.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Certified silence decreases with short-delay drive}
\label{sec:governing}

Certified silence tracks $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ within a single network, across architectures and across penalty weights. Re-certifying one trained network at three horizons, with its weights fixed, gives the ordering the decomposition predicts. At $K=2$ no tap binds and 99.2\% of the oracle fraction is certified; at $K=4$ only the $d=2$ tap binds ($R_{\mathrm{short}}=0.275$) and certification reaches 94.9\% of oracle; at $K=8$ the $d=4$ tap enters the binding set, $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ rises to 6.307 and certification falls to 0.2\%. The usable horizon of an unconstrained delay set is therefore set by its second-smallest delay.

Table~\ref{tab:governing} compares six architectures spanning a thirty-fold range of $R_{\mathrm{short}}$. They fall into two groups: the four with $R_{\mathrm{short}} \geq 3.06$ certify 0.0--6.3\% of oracle and the two with $R_{\mathrm{short}} \leq 0.26$ certify 95.0--95.6\%. Total recurrent excitation does not order them: the unit-delay network holds 7.91 and certifies nothing, while the constrained learnable-delay network holds 5.00 and certifies 95.6\% (figure~\ref{fig:evid}a,b, where the six architectures are numbered in the order of table~\ref{tab:governing}). Across 23 networks spanning a hundredfold range of $R_{\mathrm{short}}$, including the width-scaling networks of section~\ref{sec:scale} and the recipe and time-constant variants listed in the supplementary material, the rank correlation is $\rho=-0.804$. Several of these networks are variants of one training recipe; leaving out each of the eight network families in turn gives $\rho$ between $-0.60$ and $-0.90$, and one median network per family gives $\rho=-0.83$.

\subsection{Training moves excitation into longer delays}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Training meets the bound by moving excitation from the binding tap into longer delays. Figure~\ref{fig:evid}d,e shows the per-delay drive before and after constrained fine-tuning. On SHD the binding $d=2$ drive falls from 4.97 to 0.28, by 94\%, while the $d=4$ and $d=8$ drives rise from 4.90 to 6.03 and from 5.40 to 6.46, so total excitatory mass changes by 16\%; on SSC (seed 1) the $d=2$ drive falls from 5.73 to 0.29 and total mass from 19.04 to 18.83, a change of 1\%. The $d=2$ drive lands at 0.29--0.30 on four SSC seeds and $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ at 0.253--0.260 on three SHD test seeds. On N-MNIST the binding tap falls from 3.75 to 0.13 while total excitatory mass changes from 11.82 to 10.16.

A unit-delay network has no longer tap to absorb the mass and meets the same budget by removing excitation: positive recurrent weight mass falls from 2385 to 199 and the excitation-to-inhibition ratio from 0.650 to 0.045.

With a learnable delay per synapse, training for accuracy places 33.3\% of excitatory mass in the shortest delay bin and 38.1\% in the longest, so 43.5\% falls below the horizon ($R_{\mathrm{short}}=3.06$) and the network certifies 1.9\% of oracle (figure~\ref{fig:mech}b). The penalty reduces mass below the horizon to 5.9\% and $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ to 0.246 by reweighting synapses: the unweighted mean delay moves from 5.00 to 5.39 while the excitation-weighted mean delay moves from 5.17 to 7.07. Certification reaches 95.5\% of oracle for an accuracy change of $-0.17$ points. Widening the delay range to $d\le25$ without the penalty raises certification to 15.5\% of oracle. Where a longer delay is available, training moves excitation into it; where none is, training removes excitation.

\subsection{Execution is exact, and silent-run length bounds its gain}
\label{sec:execution}

Every timed run, 1\,794 in total across both engines and both machines, produced spike trains bit-identical to the single-threaded reference.

On the shared-memory engine with 4--64 physical cores, certificates are faster by $1.10$--$1.12\times$ at $L=500\,\mu$s at every core count, and by up to $1.06\times$ at $L=100\,\mu$s; the unconstrained control gains at no setting, so the effect is attributable to training. At $L\leq20\,\mu$s the delay-derived lookahead already hides the latency, so certificates add computation without removing waiting and are slower than the baseline. The speed-up for every core count and latency, and for a two-process engine communicating over TCP, is given in the supplementary material.

Table~\ref{tab:ceiling} compares certificates with the oracle at $L=500\,\mu$s. The oracle bounds the gain at $1.53\times$ for the dense network and $1.57$--$1.71\times$ for locally connected networks: even with perfect foreknowledge, the steps in which a core spikes still force a wait. With a $K=4$ certificate the dense and local networks realise 23\% and 25\% of the available headroom at 16 cores. Training the local network against a $K=8$ certificate binds the $d=4$ tap as well; it drives both short taps below the budget ($R^{(2)}=0.07$, $R^{(4)}=0.14$) while excitation moves to the $d=8$ tap ($R^{(8)}=1.97$), certifies 95.5\% of oracle at $K=8$ for a validation change of $-0.43$ points, and realises 39\% of the headroom, $1.28\times$ at 16 cores.
The host networks separate silence frequency from silence length. With 16 cores of 32 neurons, their cores are silent in 61--66\% of core-steps, as often as in the dense and local networks (66--67\%), but their silent runs average 5.9--6.1 steps against 14.0--15.1, and only 67--68\% of their silent core-steps lie in runs longer than eight steps against 87--88\%. At 64 cores the recurrent layer is certifiably silent in 85--88\% of core-steps, and the oracle still gains at most $1.03\times$: a run removes waiting only when it outlasts the steps a core would have waited for, so the attainable gain follows run length.

The certificate unit adds no cycles to a pass, and its 16-bit form keeps the certificate sound. Figure~\ref{fig:hw}a shows the core of section~\ref{sec:hwmethods} and figure~\ref{fig:hw}b its pass schedule: after a normal pass whose certificate holds, the core skips the next $K-2$ steps and advances its neurons through the $K-1$ skipped steps in one catch-up pass. With the 16-bit certificate, normal passes take 59--69\% of core-steps, catch-up passes 11--14\%, and 21--27\% of core-steps need no neuron work. The certificate unit evaluates the neuron being updated in the same cycle, so certification adds no cycles to a pass.

With operands in units of $2^{-8}$ and six-bit decay factors, the certificate holds in 98.8\% of the core-steps certified at full precision for the local network and 96.0\% for the DCLS host layer, with no violation; in units of $2^{-4}$ the retention falls to 77\% and 57\%.

Table~\ref{tab:hw} gives the cost. The 16-bit certificate unit is 2.7$\times$ smaller than a 24-bit version routed with the same flow (supplementary material). \section{Discussion}

Here we have shown that provable silence in recurrent spiking networks with synaptic delays is limited by one quantity, and that training can control it. The delay decomposition makes $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ the only worst-case term of a $K$-step certificate, and certified silence tracks it at every level tested, from one network re-certified at three horizons to 23 networks, while total recurrent excitation does not order them. Certification costs at most a few tenths of a point of test accuracy, and on SSC accuracy improved (section~\ref{sec:accuracy}).

Training meets the bound by redistributing excitation across delays: the binding tap loses most of its drive while the longer taps gain, and total excitatory mass changes little (section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). A unit-delay network has no longer tap and meets the same budget by removing excitation, so delays of at least two steps are what allow certification while recurrent excitation is retained. With a learnable delay per synapse the redistribution is a reweighting of existing synapses toward longer delays. Because $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ scales with fan-in, bounded fan-in keeps networks certifiable as they grow, and the measured coefficient $R \approx 0.0098 \times \mathrm{fan\text{-}in}$ estimates the fan-in at which a target certification level is reached.
\paragraph{Relation to lookahead in parallel simulation.} The prevailing approach to lookahead in spiking simulation takes it from the network's structure: the minimum synaptic delay bounds how far a process may advance \cite{fujimoto,nest}, and topology-aware mapping lengthens that bound by placing long-delay connections between nodes \cite{lober}. Our results extend this approach to the network's state. A certificate adds a state-dependent lookahead to the delay-derived one, and the training objective shapes a network so that this lookahead exists. Its execution gain is bounded by how long cores stay silent relative to the interconnect latency, which the oracle reads from a network's spike trains before any timing experiment. The share of the oracle bound that the certificate realises grows with the trained horizon (table~\ref{tab:ceiling}). The host networks are silent as often as the networks trained here, in runs less than half as long, and their bound is close to one; the attainable gain follows the length of silent runs, which can be measured from spike trains before deployment.

\paragraph{Implications for neuromorphic hardware.} In hardware the certificate reuses the reads of the neuron update and adds no cycles to a pass. A certified core advances its skipped steps exactly in one catch-up pass and removes state-memory traffic in 21--27\% of core-steps, and directed rounding lets the certificate run in 16-bit arithmetic without losing soundness. On SpiNNaker, certificates computed from the weights the chip stores hold on every window, so the certificate carries over to an existing neuromorphic system when it is computed in that system's arithmetic. For processors with time-stepped cores the results amount to a design procedure: synaptic delays of at least two steps, bounded fan-in, a certificate penalty during training, and a certificate unit beside the neuron update.

\paragraph{Relation to cortical delay structure.} The regime in which certificates work resembles the delay structure of cortex, where local connections conduct within a fraction of a millisecond, long-range projections impose delays of tens of milliseconds, and most neurons are quiet most of the time \cite{conduction,sparsefiring}.

\paragraph{Outlook.} Three extensions follow directly. Deploying certificate-based execution on time-stepped neuromorphic processors such as Loihi 2 \cite{loihi2runtime} and SpiNNaker2 \cite{spinnaker2chip} would measure the trade between certificate computation and avoided waiting under their own costs. Extending certificates to other neuron models requires a monotone bound on the membrane over the horizon; the delay decomposition itself does not depend on the neuron model. Training objectives that lengthen silent runs would raise the execution bound that the oracle measures.

\paragraph{Scope.} The evidence covers adaptive leaky integrate-and-fire neurons with fixed delay sets of up to eight steps and learnable delays, on speech and event-vision data. Execution was timed on general-purpose processors with emulated per-message latency. The hardware figures are post-route values for a design that has not been fabricated.

Overall, this work identifies where excitation sits across delays as the property that limits how far a recurrent spiking network can run ahead of silent neighbours, and gives the means to measure it, train for it and check it in hardware.

\section{Conclusion}

Provable silence in recurrent spiking networks with synaptic delays is limited by the excitatory drive that arrives within the certificate horizon. Training moves that drive into longer delays at small cost in accuracy, also inside published architectures, so certified silence becomes a design target for training. The execution gain it allows is set by the length of silent runs, which can be read from a network's spike trains before deployment, and the certificate is cheap enough to compute beside the neuron update in narrow arithmetic and holds on an existing neuromorphic system when computed from the weights it stores.
