\begin{abstract}
Cortical neurons are silent most of the time, and the axonal delays that connect them span more than two orders of magnitude. Multi-core neuromorphic systems inherit the sparse activity but not its benefit: a core may skip waiting for a neighbour only when that neighbour's silence is established \emph{in advance}, and observed silence carries no such guarantee. We show that provable silence in a recurrent spiking network is governed by where excitation sits in time. Writing the certificate for a horizon of $K$ steps makes the dependence explicit: a synapse of delay $d$ supplies the step under test from a time step that is already computed whenever $d \geq K$, so only synapses with $d < K$ require a worst-case bound. The binding quantity is $R_{\mathrm{short}}$, the excitatory drive carried by short delays, and not total recurrent excitation. Across sixteen networks spanning a hundredfold range of $R_{\mathrm{short}}$, certified silence declines monotonically with it (Spearman $\rho=-0.844$): all five below $R_{\mathrm{short}}=0.30$ reach 95--98\% of the achievable maximum, all seven above 2.50 reach 0--6\%, and the three in between fall 90.0\%, 84.9\%, 34.6\% in exact order of $R_{\mathrm{short}}$, while the same networks ordered by total excitation show no such relation. Two consequences follow. A network with multi-tap delays can satisfy a given $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ by relocating excitation into longer delays: training with a certificate penalty cuts drive on the binding tap by 94\% while total excitatory mass changes by 1--16\%, and the resulting networks certify 56.0\% of core-steps on the Heidelberg Spiking Digits at an accuracy change of $+0.24$ points against matched controls over three test seeds, and 61.5\% on Spiking Speech Commands at $+3.03$ points over four test seeds with the recipe transferred unchanged. No soundness violation occurred in any run. Training for accuracy works against the property rather than for it: when per-synapse delays are free to move they saturate both ends of their range, placing 43.5\% of excitatory mass below the horizon, and the unconstrained network certifies 1.9\% of its achievable maximum. The penalty does not relocate delays but reweights synapses, raising the excitation-weighted mean delay from 5.2 to 7.1 steps and recovering 95.5\% at a cost of 0.17 accuracy points. The property is governed by fan-in rather than by network size: with fan-in held at 96 inputs, certification holds from 512 to 2048 neurons and improves with width, while dense recurrence at 2048 neurons drives $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ to 25 and certifies nothing. The same mechanism appears on a third dataset in a different modality, event-camera vision, where the binding tap falls by 96\% and 97.7\% of the achievable maximum is certified at an accuracy change of $+0.08$ points. Certificates license barrier-free execution that is bit-identical to lock-step, which runs 1.25--1.49$\times$ faster than lookahead-aware handshaking across 32 shared-memory cores, and across two processes pays once per-message latency exceeds roughly $350\,\mu$s.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Neuromorphic processors execute spiking neural networks as many small cores exchanging spike events \cite{spinnaker,truenorth,loihi}. Most advance in discrete algorithmic time steps, and the dependency is strict: before computing step $t+1$, a core must know which of its presynaptic neurons spiked at step $t$. This is enforced either by a global barrier \cite{truenorth,loihi} or by local handshakes between communicating cores \cite{neuroscale}. As systems scale, the cost of this synchronisation grows with them, and performance analyses of deployed neuromorphic workloads repeatedly identify communication and synchronisation as the limiting factor, ahead of arithmetic \cite{neuroscale,floorline,neurobench}.

The biological system this hardware abstracts has both properties in sharper form. Cortical neurons fire sparsely, and a large fraction are nearly silent at any moment \cite{silentbrain,sparsefiring}. Their conduction delays are correspondingly diverse: local cortical axons conduct in well under a millisecond, while cortico-cortical and cortico-thalamic axons impose delays of tens of milliseconds \cite{conduction}. That diversity is treated as a computational resource, since it supports spike-timing codes that depend on coincident arrival from differently delayed paths \cite{polychron}, and the balance of excitation against inhibition holds membrane potential below threshold for most of the time \cite{balance}. The result below connects these two facts: a network whose excitation is carried predominantly through longer delays is one whose quiescence can be \emph{verified} cheaply, with the cost of verification set by the excitation at short lags alone.

Trained recurrent spiking networks offer an apparent opportunity. Their activity is sparse --- in the networks studied here the mean firing rate is around 3\% --- so cores are idle most of the time. But observed idleness cannot be exploited. A core may skip waiting for a neighbour only if that neighbour's silence is established \emph{before} the step in question; otherwise a missed spike changes the computation. The requirement is \emph{provable} sparsity. In the networks we examine, cores are genuinely silent in roughly 59\% of core-steps, and 4\% of those are provable without intervention.

This paper identifies what provability depends on. We formulate per-core silence certificates for adaptive leaky integrate-and-fire networks with multi-tap synaptic delays, and show that the certificate at horizon $k$ must bound only those synapses whose delay is \emph{shorter} than $k$; contributions arriving through longer delays originate at time steps that are already determined and can be used exactly. Provability is therefore governed by the excitation arriving through short delays, which we denote $R_{\mathrm{short}}$.

Two consequences follow, and the experiments bear out both. Longer-delay synapses are unconstrained by a horizon-$k$ certificate, so a network can reduce $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ by moving excitation into longer delays, and certifiability need not cost accuracy. Second, a delay architecture makes that relocation possible but does not perform it: delays learned for accuracy alone saturate the short end of their range, so the drive the certificate must bound is created by the very training that makes the network accurate, and an explicit objective is needed to undo it.

Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The governing quantity.} We derive the delay decomposition of the certificate and show that certified silence declines monotonically with $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ across sixteen networks spanning 0.246 to 25.0 (Spearman $\rho=-0.844$), with no overlap between the group below 0.30 (95--98\% of the achievable maximum) and the group above 2.50 (0--6\%) (section~\ref{sec:governing}).
\item \textbf{Certification at no accuracy cost.} On the Heidelberg Spiking Digits, networks trained with a certificate penalty certify 56.0\% of core-steps at an accuracy change of $+0.24$ points relative to matched controls over three held-out test seeds; the recipe transferred to Spiking Speech Commands without retuning certifies 61.5\% at $+3.03$ points over four test seeds (sections~\ref{sec:shd}, \ref{sec:ssc}).
\item \textbf{Reallocation of excitation in time.} Training reduces drive on the binding short-delay tap by 94\% while total excitatory mass changes by 1--16\%, with the same signature on both datasets and every seed (section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item \textbf{Why an explicit objective is required.} With per-synapse learnable delays, training for accuracy drives 43.5\% of excitatory mass onto the shortest delay available and the \emph{unconstrained} network certifies 1.9\% of its achievable maximum. The penalty reweights synapses rather than relocating them, lifting the excitation-weighted mean delay from 5.17 to 7.07 steps and recovering 95.5\% for 0.17 accuracy points (section~\ref{sec:architectural}).
\item \textbf{Exact barrier-free execution and its operating regime.} Certificates permit execution that is bit-identical to lock-step. In a shared-memory engine this runs 1.25--1.49$\times$ faster than lookahead-aware local handshaking at 32 cores and slower below 16; across two processes it runs 1.13--1.18$\times$ faster once per-message latency exceeds roughly 350\,$\mu$s and slower below it, a boundary set by the waiting it removes against the computation it adds (section~\ref{sec:execution}).
\item \textbf{Scaling with bounded fan-in.} Certifiability is governed by fan-in rather than by neuron count: holding fan-in at 96 inputs, certified silence stays at 96--98\% of the achievable maximum from 512 to 2048 neurons, while dense recurrence at 2048 certifies nothing (section~\ref{sec:scale}).
\item \textbf{Generality across modality.} On event-camera vision the same reallocation occurs --- the binding tap falls 96\% --- and 97.7\% of the achievable maximum is certified at an accuracy change of $+0.08$ points over three seeds (section~\ref{sec:nmnist}).
\item \textbf{Boundary conditions.} Activity-cap bounds, faster membrane leak, restricted fan-in, increased width, and learnable per-synapse delays each leave the accuracy--certifiability frontier unimproved, which delimits where the governing relation can be acted on (section~\ref{sec:negative}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and related work}

\paragraph{Synchronisation in neuromorphic systems.} TrueNorth advances all cores on a global tick \cite{truenorth}. Loihi exchanges barrier messages between neighbouring cores that flush in-flight spikes and propagate the time-step advance \cite{loihi}. SpiNNaker runs cores against real-time timers and exchanges multicast packets \cite{spinnaker}. Recent work replaces global barriers with local, neighbour-to-neighbour handshakes, removing the linear growth of synchronisation cost with system size \cite{neuroscale}. Our certificates are complementary: they reduce how often a handshake must be waited for, leaving the handshake protocol itself unchanged.

\paragraph{Lookahead in parallel discrete-event simulation.} The problem of advancing a process without global synchronisation is long established. Conservative algorithms require \emph{lookahead} --- a guarantee that no event will arrive before some future time --- to let a process proceed safely \cite{fujimoto,chandymisra}; optimistic algorithms instead run ahead and roll back on causality errors \cite{jefferson}. Distributed spiking network simulators take their lookahead from the minimum synaptic delay, exchanging spikes only once per minimum-delay interval \cite{nest}. This is the structure we exploit: minimum delay supplies lookahead at no cost, and certificates extend it beyond what the delay alone provides. A baseline must therefore be given the delay-derived lookahead to be fair, and ours is throughout (section~\ref{sec:engines}).

\paragraph{Exploiting existing structure to reduce synchronisation.} A productive line of work reduces synchronisation cost by mapping a network onto hardware so that structure it already has is exploited. Structure-aware placement assigns whole cortical areas to compute nodes, so that the minimum delay \emph{between} nodes becomes the long inter-areal delay instead of the short intra-areal one; this raises the global exchange interval tenfold and cuts synchronisation time by 48\% at 128 MPI processes \cite{lober}. Distributed simulators set their communication interval from the global minimum delay in the same way \cite{stacs,nest}. A hardware route removes the global barrier entirely, replacing it with local advance and done handshakes between directly connected cores while preserving determinism \cite{neuroscale}. Each of these extracts the lookahead a network already possesses, from its connectivity and its delay map, and each is therefore fixed before execution begins. A silence certificate is state-dependent and extends lookahead \emph{past} that point. Our baseline is given the delay-derived lookahead in full, so the gains in section~\ref{sec:execution} are measured on top of it, and the two mechanisms compose: certificates would reduce the local handshake traffic that \cite{neuroscale} still requires, and would apply within the areas mapped by \cite{lober} as well as between them.

\paragraph{Parallelism by changing the neuron.} An alternative to exact parallel execution is to alter the neuron so that time steps decouple. Separating leaky integration from firing and removing the membrane reset turns integration into a linear filter evaluable over a whole sequence at once, giving 10--30$\times$ on the datasets used here \cite{paralif}; asynchronous layer scheduling can likewise cut spike count and latency \cite{layersync}. These methods change what is computed. Our requirement is the complementary one --- spike trains bit-identical to lock-step execution --- which bounds the attainable speed-up and preserves the trained network exactly.

\paragraph{Synaptic delays as an architectural component.} Delays are established and actively developed in spiking architectures. They can be learned jointly with weights through dilated convolutions with learnable spacings \cite{dcls}, and delayed convolutional kernels with per-delay weights improve accuracy on event-based benchmarks \cite{bamsldk}. Delay sets in this line conventionally include the shortest lags. Section~\ref{sec:governing} shows that those lags are precisely the ones carrying the cost of provability, and that omitting the unit-delay tap improves accuracy and certifiability together.

\paragraph{Bounding spiking network dynamics.} Interval-propagation bounds on spiking network state have been used to certify robustness to input perturbation \cite{certsnn}. We use the same family of bounds for a different property --- future silence over a horizon under recurrent input --- and for a different purpose, namely making a runtime scheduling decision safe. The bounded quantity here is a temporal reach, and that is what gives rise to the delay decomposition in section~\ref{sec:decomposition}.

\paragraph{Synaptic delays in trained spiking networks.} Delays are an established route to accuracy on temporal benchmarks; methods that learn a delay per synapse reach the strongest reported results on the Heidelberg datasets \cite{dcls}. Delays also change \emph{what the certificate must bound}, and we find they make networks certifiable without a dedicated objective.

\paragraph{Event-driven reformulation of sparse spiking workloads.} A complementary line of work rewrites
time-driven spiking computations in event-driven form, so that work scales with activity rather than with
time steps; a recent example reformulates eligibility propagation this way and reports exact reductions in
computation for sparse networks, with a reference implementation in NEST \cite{eprop}. That approach
reduces the work performed under a fixed dependency structure; ours alters the dependency structure, by
making a neighbour's silence provable so the dependency need not be waited on. The two are orthogonal and
could compose.

\paragraph{Activity sparsity is not provability.} Firing-rate penalties are the standard route to sparse spiking activity. We find that sparsity and certifiability are close to orthogonal: the networks with the lowest firing rates in our sweeps are not the certifiable ones, and a strong rate penalty produces sparse networks that certify under 1\% of core-steps. The quantity that governs certifiability is the worst-case drive through short delays.

\section{Theory}

\subsection{Network model}

We consider a single recurrent layer of adaptive leaky integrate-and-fire (ALIF) neurons. With membrane potential $v$, adaptation $a$, external input $I$ and spikes $s\in\{0,1\}$, neuron $i$ evolves as
\begin{eqnarray}
a_i(t) &=& \rho\, a_i(t-1) + \gamma\, s_i(t-1) \\
v_i(t) &=& \beta\, v_i(t-1) + I_i(t) + \sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}} \sum_j W^{(d)}_{ij}\, s_j(t-d) \\
s_i(t) &=& \mathbb{1}\!\left[\, v_i(t) \ge \theta + a_i(t) \,\right],
\end{eqnarray}
followed by the reset $v_i(t) \leftarrow v_i(t) - s_i(t)\,(\theta + a_i(t))$, where $\beta=\exp(-\Delta t/\tau_m)$ is the membrane decay, $\theta$ the base threshold, and $W^{(d)}$ the recurrent weight matrix for synaptic delay $d$. Setting $\mathcal{D}=\{1\}$ recovers the conventional unit-delay recurrent network. Neurons are partitioned into $P$ cores of $C$ neurons each; a core is the unit of synchronisation.

\subsection{Silence certificates}

A core issues a certificate ``silent through step $u$'' when it can establish that none of its neurons will fire at steps $t+2,\dots,u$, whatever its neighbours do. A neighbour holding such a certificate need not be waited for over that interval. Because the true threshold $\theta + a_i$ is at least $\theta$ (adaptation is non-negative), it is sound to reason against the base threshold $\theta$.

Let $R^{(d)}_i = \sum_j \max(0, W^{(d)}_{ij})$ be the worst-case excitatory drive into neuron $i$ through delay $d$, obtained when every presynaptic neuron fires. Bounding the membrane reach forward from the measured state $v_i(t+1)$ gives a sufficient condition for silence over $K$ steps. If the drive is bounded by $R_i$ at every step, the reach converges to $R_i/(1-\beta)$, so
\begin{equation}
R_i < (1-\beta)\,\theta
\label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
is sufficient for silence over an unbounded horizon. We refer to $(1-\beta)\theta$ as the \textbf{excitatory budget}. For the parameters used here ($\beta=e^{-1/2}$, $\theta=1$) the budget is $0.3935$. A finite-$K$ certificate is more permissive than (\ref{eq:budget}), because it starts from the measured membrane potential; section~\ref{sec:governing} quantifies the gap.

We evaluate certificates with a fixed-point refinement. Let $\mathcal{S}$ be a set of neurons that may fire within the window. Bounding each neuron's reach using only the drive attributable to $\mathcal{S}$ yields a possibly smaller set; iterating to a fixed point gives the certified set. This is sound for any initial superset and tightens the bound where the network is mostly quiet.

\subsection{The delay decomposition}
\label{sec:decomposition}

Which synapses a certificate must bound follows from arrival times. Consider a certificate rooted after step $t+1$ and a horizon $k\ge 1$, so the step under consideration is $\tau = t+1+k$. The contribution of a synapse with delay $d$ arrives from step
\begin{equation}
\tau - d = t + 1 + k - d .
\end{equation}
If $d \ge k$, then $\tau-d \le t+1$, which is a step that has \textbf{already been computed}. Its spikes are known, and the contribution can be evaluated exactly. Only synapses with $d < k$ draw on steps that are still in the future and therefore require a worst-case bound. Hence the quantity that must be bounded at horizon $k$ is
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{short}}(k) = \sum_{d<k} R^{(d)}_i ,
\end{equation}
and for a $K$-step certificate the binding quantity is $R_{\mathrm{short}} = \sum_{d<K} R^{(d)}$. Two corollaries follow immediately.

First, a minimum synaptic delay $d_{\min}$ yields $d_{\min}$ steps of exact, cost-free lookahead: for $k \le d_{\min}$ no synapse satisfies $d<k$, so nothing requires bounding. This is precisely the lookahead conservative parallel simulation extracts from minimum delay \cite{fujimoto,nest}, recovered here as a special case; certificates extend lookahead beyond it.

Second, and more consequentially, $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ can be reduced \emph{without reducing total excitation}, by moving excitatory mass from short to long delays. A network with delays $\mathcal{D}=\{2,4,8\}$ and $K=4$ is bound only by its $d=2$ tap; the $d=4$ and $d=8$ taps are unconstrained by the certificate. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} shows that trained networks exploit this. Figure~\ref{fig:mech} summarises the decomposition, the comparison a certificate makes, and what holding one licenses at run time.

\subsection{Enforcement}

Certificates can be made to hold by adding a penalty on the worst-case $K$-step reach, evaluated on windows in which the network is in fact silent:
\begin{equation}
L = L_{\mathrm{task}} + \lambda \cdot \mathrm{mean}\!\left[\, \mathrm{relu}\!\left(\max_k \mathrm{reach}_k - \theta\right) \cdot \mathbb{1}[\mathrm{silent}] \,\right],
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{reach}_k$ uses the exact/bounded split of section~\ref{sec:decomposition}. This is one enforcement route among several; section~\ref{sec:architectural} shows why some route is needed, since optimising accuracy alone moves excitation toward precisely the short lags the certificate must bound.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Datasets and protocol}

We use the Heidelberg Spiking Digits (SHD; 20 classes, 8\,156 training and 2\,264 test samples) and Spiking Speech Commands (SSC; 35 classes, 75\,466 training samples) \cite{shd}. Input spikes over 700 channels are binned into $T=100$ time steps spanning 1.4\,s.

\textbf{Selection and confirmation are strictly separated.} SHD's test set is dominated by two speakers absent from training (81.3\% of test samples come from speakers 4 and 5, which never appear in training), so a random validation split is not representative of the test condition: in earlier work on this project a configuration that won on a random split lost 4.5 accuracy points on test. All architecture and hyperparameter choices in this paper were therefore made on a \textbf{speaker-disjoint validation set} (training speakers 3 and 6 held out, 1\,169 samples), and the resulting recipe was \textbf{frozen} before any test evaluation. Reported headline results come from the frozen recipe evaluated once per run on the test set with \textbf{fresh random seeds} not used during selection. Holding out two speakers costs the control about 2.1 points of absolute test accuracy, so validation figures are systematically lower than test figures and the two are never mixed.

Every accuracy cost is computed \textbf{per seed against that seed's own matched control} --- a control with identical architecture, schedule and seed, trained without the certificate penalty --- never against a pooled control mean.

\subsection{Architectures}

All networks have $H=512$ recurrent ALIF neurons partitioned into 16 cores of 32 neurons (and 4--32 cores for the execution study), $\beta=e^{-1/2}$, $\theta=1$, $\rho=e^{-14/200}$, $\gamma=0.02$. We compare: a conventional unit-delay network ($\mathcal{D}=\{1\}$); multi-tap networks with $\mathcal{D}=\{1,2,4,8\}$ and $\mathcal{D}=\{2,4,8\}$; and a network with a learnable delay per synapse, parameterised as $D_{ij}\in[2,8]$ via a sigmoid and interpolated onto integer taps by a triangular kernel, $W^{(k)}_{ij} = W_{ij}\cdot\mathrm{relu}(1-|D_{ij}-k|)$. The interpolation kernel sums to unity per synapse, so total synaptic strength is preserved; the parameter count is $2H^2$ irrespective of the delay range, against $|\mathcal{D}|H^2$ for fixed taps.

\subsection{Training}

All six architectures of table~\ref{tab:governing} are trained at a common budget --- 150 epochs with identical augmentation --- so that the comparison isolates delay structure from training effort, and each is run on three seeds. Surrogate-gradient training with AdamW (learning rate $2\times10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), batch size 128, cosine schedule, 150 epochs. Augmentation applies circular shifts in time and channel ($\pm10$ bins) and masks a random block of up to 40 channels and up to 10 time steps. Constrained networks are obtained by fine-tuning from the matched control for 150 epochs at learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$ with the penalty weight ramped linearly over the first half of training; $\lambda=1.0$ unless stated. On SSC the recipe is transferred unchanged, with the epoch count matched by \textbf{gradient steps} rather than epochs (8\,250 steps, i.e.\ 14 SSC epochs), since SSC is roughly nine times larger; this is the only adaptation made, and it was declared before the runs.

\subsection{Certification measurement}

We report the \textbf{certified core fraction}: the proportion of core-steps for which a whole core is provably silent over the next $K=4$ steps. The natural ceiling is the \textbf{oracle fraction}, the proportion of core-steps in which the core is \emph{actually} silent over the same window; a certified fraction cannot exceed it. We therefore report certification both absolutely and as a percentage of oracle. A \textbf{violation} is a certified window in which some neuron does in fact fire; violations are counted exhaustively on every evaluation and any non-zero count invalidates the corresponding result.

\subsection{Certifiability is set by fan-in, not by width}
\label{sec:scale}

$R_i$ sums the positive recurrent weights into neuron $i$, so it scales with the number of inputs that neuron receives. Under dense recurrence that number is $H$, and $R$ therefore grows with width: the control's mean rises from 4.78 at $H=512$ to 8.72 at $H=1024$ against a budget fixed at 0.3935 by the neuron model. This predicts that certifiability degrades with width under dense recurrence and is \emph{independent} of width whenever fan-in is bounded. We test that with a ring connectivity in which each core reads only its immediate neighbours, holding fan-in at $(2r+1)C = 96$ neurons at every width.

Table~\ref{tab:scale} separates the two. Under bounded fan-in, $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ does not grow with width --- it falls, from 1.221 to 0.877 as $H$ quadruples --- and certification holds at 96.3--98.5\% of oracle at every width, with accuracy between 85.1 and 86.5. Under dense recurrence at the same $H=2048$, $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ reaches 25.007, sixty-four times the budget, nothing certifies, and accuracy falls to 74.25.

Two further points follow from the control rows. Certification \emph{improves} with width at fixed fan-in, from 34.6\% to 90.0\% of oracle, because wider networks at constant fan-in are quieter. And at $H=2048$ the unconstrained control already reaches 90.0\% of oracle with no certificate penalty applied: bounded fan-in can supply the margin on its own. This is the one architectural route to certifiability the evidence supports, and it acts through connectivity rather than through the delay distribution, which section~\ref{sec:architectural} shows moves the wrong way under training.

The declared prediction that control $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ would stay within $\pm25$\% of its $H=512$ value holds at $H=1024$ ($-17.6$\%) and is missed at $H=2048$ ($-28.2$\%), in the direction of lower drive and therefore easier certification. We report the miss as declared.

\subsection{A third modality}
\label{sec:nmnist}

The Heidelberg datasets are both audio and share a generating pipeline, so the transfer in section~\ref{sec:ssc} does not test a change of modality. We therefore repeated the procedure on N-MNIST, an event-camera recording of handwritten digits through three saccades, with the recipe transferred unchanged and epochs matched by gradient steps as before. Across three seeds the control certifies 29.2\% of oracle and the constrained networks 97.7\%, at test accuracies of 98.55\% and 98.64\% respectively --- an accuracy change of $+0.08$ points --- with no soundness violation.

The mechanism is the same one. Drive on the binding $d=2$ tap falls from 3.75 to 0.13, a reduction of 96.5\%, while total excitatory mass changes from 11.82 to 10.16, a reduction of 14\%; the binding tap lands at 0.13 on all three seeds. The signature that appears on spoken digits and spoken commands appears unchanged on event-camera vision.

\subsection{Exact multi-core execution}
\label{sec:engines}

Two engines were implemented in C++20. The first is a shared-memory engine with one thread per core and emulated interconnect latency $L$, in which published data becomes visible to other cores $L$\,$\mu$s after publication. The second partitions cores across separate operating-system processes exchanging fixed-size frames over TCP with Nagle's algorithm disabled, so that latency, system-call cost and scheduling are those the operating system actually imposes.

In both engines the \textbf{baseline is given the lookahead the delays provide}: to compute step $t+1$ a core waits only for neighbour spikes up to step $t+1-d_{\min}$, which with $d_{\min}=2$ leaves every core permanently one step ahead with no certificate at all. Comparing certificates against a delay-naive handshake would attribute to the method a speed-up that belongs to the delays.

Both engines verify spike trains \textbf{bit-for-bit against a single-threaded reference} on every run. Any mismatch invalidates the timings of that run. All reported timings were taken on an otherwise idle machine; two earlier measurement attempts were discarded because concurrent workloads were detected, and in one case the idle check itself contained a race.

\section{Results}

\subsection{$R_{\mathrm{short}}$ governs certifiability}
\label{sec:governing}

The decomposition fixes, for a given horizon, exactly which synapses constrain a certificate: a tap of delay $d$ contributes to certificate step $k$ from time step $t+1+k-d$, which is already computed whenever $d \geq k$. For $\mathcal{D}=\{2,4,8\}$ a horizon of 4 therefore binds the $d=2$ tap alone, and a horizon of 8 brings $d=4$ into the binding set as well.

Re-certifying one set of trained weights at three horizons, with the weights and every other setting held fixed, follows that prediction. At $K=2$ the binding set is empty, $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ is exactly zero, and 99.2\% of the oracle fraction is certified by construction. At $K=4$ only the $d=2$ tap binds, $R_{\mathrm{short}}=0.275$, and certification reaches 94.9\% of oracle. At $K=8$ the $d=4$ tap enters the binding set, $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ rises 23-fold to 6.307, and certification falls to 0.2\% of oracle. The usable horizon is therefore bounded by the \textbf{second-smallest synaptic delay}, which makes $K=4$ the largest horizon this delay set supports. None of the 18 runs produced a soundness violation.

Certification separates sharply on $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ and is unrelated to total excitation (figure~\ref{fig:evid}a,b). Pooling these with the seven networks of table~\ref{tab:scale} gives thirteen spanning 0.246 to 25.0, over which the rank correlation between $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ and certified fraction is $\rho=-0.879$. The relation is a steep sigmoid rather than a proportionality: the five networks below 0.30 certify 95.0--98.5\%, the five above 3.06 certify 0.0--6.3\%, and the three in the transition certify 90.0\%, 84.9\% and 34.6\% in exact order of $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ (0.877, 1.006, 1.221). Rank inversions occur only inside the two plateaus, where the spread is at the level of seed noise.

Three further networks, trained under a deliberately different recipe during an unsuccessful attempt to raise absolute accuracy (section~\ref{sec:negative}), fall on the same relation: at $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ of 2.50, 2.77 and 2.91 they certify 1.5\%, 1.7\% and 0.9\% of oracle. Pooling them gives $\rho=-0.844$ over sixteen networks. They matter for two reasons. They are trained with batch normalisation of the feedforward current, so their membrane distribution differs from every other network reported here, and the relation is therefore not an artifact of the common schedule under which table~\ref{tab:governing} was collected. And they extend the upper plateau downward: seven networks spanning $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ from 2.50 to 8.74, a 3.5-fold range, all certify between 0.0\% and 6.3\%. The boundary therefore lies between roughly 1.2 and 2.5, about three to six times the budget. The finite-horizon certificate is thus more permissive than the unbounded-horizon condition (\ref{eq:budget}), as expected, since it starts from the measured membrane potential.

Total recurrent excitation carries none of this. The unit-delay network holds 7.91 of it and certifies nothing, while the constrained learnable-delay network holds less, 7.22, and certifies 97.6\%; the architecture with the most total excitation of all, 18.40, certifies 3.8\%. The pair differing only in $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ (4.813 against 0.261) differs by a factor of fifteen in certified fraction at equal total drive. Reproducibility across seeds is close: $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ lands within 0.253--0.275 on the constrained three-tap networks and 1.595--1.624 on the learnable-delay controls.

Core size sets how much silence is available to certify and leaves the certificate's tightness intact. Partitioning the same network into 32, 64 and 128 neurons per core certifies 56.28\%, 47.53\% and 39.92\% of core-steps against oracle fractions of 59.3\%, 50.4\% and 42.1\% --- 94.9\%, 94.2\% and 94.7\% of what is achievable. Larger cores are \emph{actually} silent less often, since certification requires every neuron in a core to be quiet; the gap between certificate and oracle does not widen.

\subsection{Training for accuracy concentrates excitation at short delays}
\label{sec:architectural}

Section~\ref{sec:governing} establishes that $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ governs certified silence, and section~\ref{sec:mechanism} shows that a certificate penalty reduces it by relocating excitation into longer delays. A natural question is whether an architecture with free delays reaches a low $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ on its own, making the penalty unnecessary. It does not. Training for accuracy moves the delay distribution in the opposite direction.

We replace the fixed taps with a per-synapse continuous delay $D_{ij} = d_{\min} + (d_{\max}-d_{\min})\,\sigma(\rho_{ij})$ learned jointly with the weights \cite{dcls}, with $d_{\min}=2$, $d_{\max}=8$ and $\rho$ initialised uniformly on $[-2,2]$. Delay positions are trained at 100 times the weight learning rate through a Gaussian interpolation kernel whose width is annealed from $(d_{\max}-d_{\min})/2$ to 0.5 over the first quarter of training, following \cite{dcls}; both are necessary, for the reason given at the end of this section.

\textbf{Learned delays saturate both ends of their range.} In the unconstrained network 33.3\% of excitatory mass lands in the shortest delay bin and 38.1\% in the longest, with the interior roughly thirty times lower (figure~\ref{fig:mech}b). The distribution is bimodal at the boundaries rather than spread. Because one of those boundaries lies below the horizon, 43.5\% of excitatory mass falls in $d<K$, giving $R_{\mathrm{short}}=3.06$ against a budget of 0.3935. The network certifies 1.02\% of core-steps, 1.9\% of oracle.

\textbf{The penalty reweights synapses rather than relocating them.} Applying it cuts drive on the shortest bin by 89\% and mass below the horizon from 43.5\% to 5.9\%, with $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ falling from 3.06 to 0.246, a factor of twelve. The mechanism is visible in two means that move differently: the unweighted mean delay shifts only from 5.00 to 5.39, while the excitation-weighted mean delay rises from 5.17 to 7.07. Delays stay roughly where they are and the weights on the short ones are removed, which is the cheaper of the two routes --- the accuracy cost is 0.17 points, from 86.14\% to 85.97\%, and certification reaches 52.30\% of core-steps, 95.5\% of oracle, with no soundness violation.

This is the sharpest available separation of the architecture's contribution from the objective's. The two networks share an architecture, an initialisation, a seed and a schedule, and differ only in the penalty; between them certified silence moves by a factor of fifty.

\textbf{A methodological note, because the error is easy to repeat.} An earlier version of this work reported the opposite: that the unconstrained learnable-delay network certifies 97.4\% of oracle and the penalty is therefore optional. That measurement came from an implementation in which delay positions were trained at the weight learning rate and interpolated with a fixed triangular kernel of half-width~1. Under those conditions a delay receives gradient only from the two adjacent taps and cannot traverse the range, so the delays stayed at their initialisation, whose mean of 5.04 is exactly $d_{\min} + (d_{\max}-d_{\min})/2$. A uniform delay distribution places little mass at any particular lag and is therefore easy to certify, so the defect presented as a favourable result. We record it because the learning rate ratio and the annealing schedule are reported as hyperparameters in the source we followed \cite{dcls}, which understates their role: they are what makes the delays learnable at all, and a network whose delays silently fail to train is more certifiable than one whose delays succeed.

Across two processes the outcome is set by what a message costs. The engine prices messages explicitly: each frame carries its send time and becomes observable to the receiver only after a per-message latency $L$; a frame that is never sent therefore costs nothing, and $L$ prices communication while leaving computation untouched. A certificate also licenses a rank to stop transmitting, since a published certificate already proves the omitted steps silent. This is sound and it does reduce traffic, from $99.0$ to $71.1$ frames per sample ($1.39\times$), and it was slower than transmitting every step at every latency tested. \subsection{Boundary conditions}
\label{sec:negative}

Five routes suggested by the governing relation were tested and did not improve the accuracy--certifiability frontier. Each delimits where the relation can be acted on.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Tightening the bound with activity caps.} The worst-case bound assumes every presynaptic neuron fires, whereas measured simultaneous activity is 0.98 of 32 neurons per core on average (99.9th percentile 7, maximum 10) --- a pessimism factor above twenty. Yet a sound ``at most $k$ per presynaptic core'' bound tightens $R$ by only 1.1--4.1$\times$, because the positive weight distribution is heavy-tailed; even $k=1$ leaves $R$ at 1.125, still 2.9 times the budget, certifying 0\% of neurons. Activity-cap certificates therefore leave the frontier unchanged.
\item \textbf{Enlarging the budget via faster membrane leak.} The budget $(1-\beta)\theta$ grows as the membrane time constant falls, by 1.61$\times$ at $\tau_m/2$ and 2.20$\times$ at $\tau_m/4$. This raises certification but costs base accuracy, and on the delay architecture it is superseded: once delays supply the margin, the budget is no longer binding.
\item \textbf{Restricting fan-in.} $R$ is almost exactly linear in fan-in ($R \approx 0.0098 \times \mathrm{fan\text{-}in}$ across a tenfold range), so local connectivity reduces it proportionally and makes $R$ width-independent. Certification duly rises, but absolute accuracy falls by about 8 points and additional width does not recover it (65.95\% at $H=512$, 67.24\% at $H=1024$, 64.59\% at $H=2048$).
\item \textbf{Per-synapse learnable delays for accuracy.} They did \textbf{not} improve accuracy: 86.14\% against the fixed-tap architecture's 87.25\%. The parameter-efficiency hypothesis --- that fixed taps were limited by their $|\mathcal{D}|H^2$ parameters --- is not supported. They are also harder to certify without a penalty, not easier (section~\ref{sec:architectural}).
\item \textbf{Longer training.} On the delay architecture 300 epochs is worse than 150 (84.69\% against 87.25\% for controls), the reverse of the delay-free case.
\item \textbf{Importing the training recipe of a stronger architecture.} The best published SHD results come from feedforward networks with learnable delays, so we transferred that recipe wholesale --- dropout 0.4, batch normalisation, one-cycle schedule, batch 256, arctangent surrogate, input channels binned 700 to 140, and a low-rate fine-tuning tail. It gives 76.13\% against our unchanged 87.25\%. Removing ingredients one at a time locates almost all of the loss in \textbf{dropout}: without it the same recipe reaches 86.23\%, recovering 10.1 of the 11.1 points, while binning accounts for 2.4 and the remainder is within single-seed spread. The explanation is structural rather than a matter of rate. Dropout in the source architecture is applied between the layers of a feedforward stack, where each layer resamples its input; applied to the drive of a single recurrent layer it is integrated into a state that persists, so the perturbation accumulates over time steps instead of being averaged away. Recipes developed on feedforward delay architectures should not be assumed to transfer to recurrent ones.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A design rule}

The results support a concrete rule. Certifiability is governed by $R_{\mathrm{short}}$, the worst-case excitatory drive arriving through synapses with delay below the certificate horizon. To obtain provable silence:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Use synaptic delays of at least two steps and omit the unit-delay tap.} This guarantees two steps of exact lookahead and improves accuracy and certifiability together, so there is no trade-off to manage.
\item \textbf{Do not expect learned delays to spread themselves.} Delays optimised for accuracy saturate both ends of their range and leave 43.5\% of excitatory mass below the horizon. A delay architecture supplies somewhere for excitation to go, not the pressure to send it there; that pressure is the certificate penalty (section~\ref{sec:architectural}).
\item \textbf{Where a penalty is needed, expect reallocation.} Networks satisfy the constraint by moving excitation into longer delays; total excitatory mass need not fall and accuracy need not suffer.
\item \textbf{For hardware, prefer fine-grained cores.} Certification requires a whole core to be silent, so coverage rises as cores shrink (44.5\% at 4 cores to 71.0\% at 32).
\item \textbf{Compare the waiting removed against the certificate's computation.} Both are measurable, and their crossing predicts whether barrier-free execution pays on a given interconnect: at 32 cores the certificate costs $5.1$\,ms per sample and wins once per-message latency exceeds roughly $350\,\mu$s (section~\ref{sec:execution}).
\end{enumerate}

Since $R$ is linear in fan-in with a measurable coefficient, the maximum affordable fan-in for a target certification level can be computed in advance.

\subsection{Relation to cortical delay structure}

The regime in which the method works is the one cortex appears to occupy. Certification requires excitatory drive at short lags to sit below the budget, and leaves drive at longer lags unconstrained. Trained without a certificate penalty, our learned-delay networks do not settle there: they place 43.5\% of excitatory mass below the horizon at an excitation-weighted mean delay of 5.17 steps. The penalty moves them into the regime, to 5.9\% and 7.07 steps (figure~\ref{fig:mech}b). Cortical connectivity shows the same asymmetry in stronger form: dense local recurrence conducts within a fraction of a millisecond, while the long-range projections carrying inter-areal excitation impose delays hundreds of times longer \cite{conduction}, and the resulting networks are quiescent the great majority of the time \cite{silentbrain,sparsefiring}. We make no claim that cortex computes certificates. The statement the evidence supports is narrower and still useful: provable quiescence is cheap exactly where excitation is carried by broadly distributed delays, which is the biological regime, so a neuromorphic system that inherits that delay structure inherits the provability along with it. It also sharpens the design rule above --- delay diversity is usually motivated by its computational role \cite{polychron}, and the evidence here gives it a second, independent justification.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Absolute accuracy is below the state of the art.} Our strongest network reaches 87.59\% on SHD against roughly 96\% for the best reported architectures, and 68.20\% on SSC. The route indicated by the parameter-count evidence --- per-synapse learnable delays --- was tested and did not close it (section~\ref{sec:negative}). Depth, time resolution and training protocol were held fixed, so the remaining gap is attributable to those and is unresolved here. The evidence therefore establishes the relation over the accuracy range 85--88\% on SHD, and its behaviour at state-of-the-art accuracy would require an architecture we did not evaluate. Within the range tested the trend is favourable: the better of our two delay architectures is also the more certifiable.

\textbf{The distributed speed-up holds only above a latency threshold, measured on one configuration.} Across two processes certificates win by 1.13--1.18$\times$ above a per-message latency of roughly $350\,\mu$s and lose below it (section~\ref{sec:execution}). Two processes is the weakest distributed configuration and the engine supports no more, so both the threshold and the speed-up are established for that configuration alone; the break-even latency in particular depends on the certificate's computational cost, which scales with the number of cores per rank. Latencies above $350\,\mu$s describe commodity networked hosts and not a datacentre fabric, where microsecond round-trips would place the configuration on the losing side. The shared-memory speed-up (1.25--1.49$\times$ at 32 cores) is a separate many-core result and the two should be quoted separately. Message skipping was implemented and measured, and it does not help (section~\ref{sec:execution}).

\textbf{Latency in the shared-memory engine is emulated}, and no neuromorphic hardware was used. The TCP engine removes the emulation at the cost of supporting only two ranks.

\textbf{Scale is bounded by fan-in, not by neuron count.} An earlier version of this work reported that certifiability degrades with network size, on the evidence of a failure at $H=1024$ under dense recurrence. Section~\ref{sec:scale} shows that attribution was wrong: the governing quantity scales with fan-in, which dense recurrence ties to $H$, and once fan-in is bounded certification holds to 2048 neurons and improves with width. The correct statement of the limit is therefore architectural rather than numerical --- the method requires bounded fan-in, which is also the regime with the largest measured speed-ups --- and the untested region is large fan-in, not large networks. Execution experiments remain at up to 32 cores, and the core-granularity effect in table~\ref{tab:speed} still indicates that systems with many neurons per core certify whole cores less often.

\textbf{The transition region is sampled by three networks.} The relation between $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ and certified silence is monotone across the thirteen networks measured and ordered correctly inside the transition, but that transition --- the interval from roughly 0.3 to 2.5, over which certified fraction falls from near-oracle to near-zero --- contains only three of them, all locally connected and differing in width. We therefore establish the \emph{existence} and approximate location of the transition, not its shape, and a design margin should be taken against its lower edge rather than its midpoint.

An earlier version of this work reported the relation as loose in this region, on the evidence of an unconstrained learnable-delay network that certified 97.4\% of oracle at $R_{\mathrm{short}}=1.595$ while a locally connected network at the lower value 1.221 certified 34.6\%. That network is the mis-trained one described in section~\ref{sec:architectural}; at its corrected value of 3.058 it certifies 1.9\%, which is in order. We also tested whether the dispersion of $R_i$ across neurons supplied a missing term, since a core certifies only when every neuron in it stays below threshold, and it does not: the coefficient of variation is 0.43--0.56 for every network measured, local and dense alike, and certified fraction correlates with mean $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ at $-0.85$ against $-0.13$ for dispersion. Both results are reported because the first was a false lead generated by our own implementation defect and the second is a genuine negative.

A predictive account of certified fraction --- rather than an ordering --- would need the membrane and activity distributions as well as $R_{\mathrm{short}}$, since a certificate starts from the measured potential and is tightened by a fixed-point refinement over the neurons that may fire. We have not attempted one.

\textbf{Statistical scope.} Headline results rest on three (SHD) and four (SSC) test seeds. The architecture comparison of table~\ref{tab:governing} rests on three seeds per architecture, all trained at a common budget, and the separation it reports exceeds the seed spread by an order of magnitude; $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ itself reproduces to about $\pm0.01$.

\textbf{One property, one optimisation.} We certify silence and use it to skip synchronisation. Whether the same approach --- training a network so that a runtime guarantee becomes provable --- extends to other properties and other exact optimisations is open, and is in our view the most interesting direction this work suggests.

\section{Conclusion}

This study establishes an operating relation between the temporal distribution of recurrent excitation and the provability of silence. The governing quantity is $R_{\mathrm{short}}$, the worst-case excitatory drive arriving through synapses whose delay falls below the certificate horizon; certified silence declines monotonically with it across a hundredfold range, reaching 95--98\% of the achievable maximum below $R_{\mathrm{short}}=0.30$ and 0--6\% above 2.50.

The relation is actionable because the horizon partitions the synapses. Delays at or beyond the horizon arrive from steps already computed, so they carry no constraint, and a network can satisfy a given $R_{\mathrm{short}}$ by redistributing excitation in time. Trained networks do so: drive on the binding tap falls by 94\% while total excitatory mass changes by 1--16\%, with the same signature on both datasets and every seed, and the resulting networks certify 56\% of core-steps on SHD and 61\% on SSC with no soundness violation in any run. Where a delay distribution already places little mass below the horizon, the property holds with no training objective at all.

Two boundaries delimit the result. The relation was established over an accuracy range of 85--88\% on SHD, and its behaviour in networks at the reported state of the art remains open. The execution benefit is conditional, and the condition is quantitative: barrier-free execution runs faster than lookahead-aware handshaking across 32 shared-memory cores, and across two processes it wins once the waiting a certificate removes exceeds the $5.1$\,ms it costs to compute, which occurs above a per-message latency of roughly $350\,\mu$s. The quantity that governs provability is measurable before any execution experiment is run, which allows a target certification level to be converted into a constraint on fan-in and delay structure at design time.
